## 1. Track-by-track meta-analysis

### ai-foundations (6 lessons: 4 published, 2 draft)

The track's foundational claim, that the real risk is a peer using AI to outproduce you rather than AI replacing you outright, is unchallenged by anything in September's scan. Lesson 01 ("What Is the AI Wage Gap") rests on the premise that closing the gap is about building systems, not chasing cost efficiency, and this month's pricing news reinforces rather than complicates that: Anthropic made its introductory Sonnet 5 rate permanent rather than raising it as scheduled (M1 Sweep 1 item 4), and open-weight models keep closing the frontier cost gap, DeepSeek-V4-Pro and Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Flash-Next both shipped in August at a fraction of flagship pricing (M1 Sweep 1 item 8). Falling and stabilizing AI cost is exactly what the lesson's thesis predicts: the constraint was never the tool's price, it was the system built around it.

The track's newest lesson, `06-what-is-ai-fluency-for-a-busy-executive.mdx` (draft, built this cycle directly from last month's recommended gap fill), gets a strong, fresh confirmation this month rather than a contradiction: SHRM's HR Technology Trends report finds 52% of workers are not getting the AI training they need and 43% of managers feel poorly or not-at-all equipped to deliver it (M1 Sweep 5 item 7), which is precisely the vacuum a plain-language, four-competency, non-technical fluency framework is built to fill. The lesson does not currently cite this statistic; it should, since it is the single sharpest piece of evidence this month for why "get AI fluent" needs the operational definition this lesson provides.

Lesson 04 ("Is AI Going to Take My Executive Job?", draft) holds without correction. Its central move, separating the displacement question from the relevance question, is not touched by anything in September's scan.

A genuinely new, currently unused piece of evidence surfaced this month that speaks directly to this track's emotional register: MIT Sloan reports that clients rate AI advisers as more competent than human ones specifically when the details being discussed are embarrassing, because AI does not judge, a pattern that held across financial, medical, career and tech domains (M1 Sweep 5 item 5). M1 itself flags this as mapping directly onto the site's "quietly ashamed executive" ICP thesis. No lesson in this track currently makes the case, explicitly, for why a leader who feels too embarrassed to admit "I don't understand this yet" to a colleague or a coach might find it easier to be honest with Claude first. This is a real, currently unaddressed opening; see Section 4, Gap 5.

Cheryl Yuran's on-record framing of AI anxiety, "it rarely looks like someone openly refusing to use a tool... more often, it shows up as hesitation and overcompensation" (M1 Sweep 6 item 1), is a strong, quotable, real-executive anchor for exactly the rung-1 anxious reader this track's early lessons are written for, and is not currently used anywhere in the curriculum.

### how-ai-works (4 lessons, all published)

The core technical model this track teaches, prediction rather than truth-checking, tokens, the context window as working memory, the training cutoff, is not touched by anything in this month's scan, same as August. Lesson 01 ("What an LLM Actually Is") remains accurate and does not need correction.

One genuine enrichment opportunity: a formal paper this month proves that summarizing an agent's accumulated context is mathematically more efficient than selectively dropping older parts, and explains that long AI conversations degrade because clutter makes the model reason worse even with room to spare in the context window, not because the window filled up (M1 Sweep 2 item 4). Lesson 01 already explains the context window correctly as a fixed-capacity working memory, but it does not yet explain the more specific, currently unaddressed behavior this research names: a long conversation degrading gradually is a distinct phenomenon from a conversation simply running out of room, and the practical fix (start fresh, or use the tool's own summarize/compact feature) is not currently in the lesson. This is a real, citable enhancement, not a correction.

### prompt-engineering (3 lessons, all published)

Lesson 03 ("Advanced Prompting Patterns"), read in full this cycle, remains a genuinely strong lesson: chain-of-thought, few-shot and structured role-play are all still current, useful technique, and nothing in September's scan contradicts any of the three patterns. Notably, the lesson's own Pattern 3 (structured adversarial role-play, having AI argue the strongest case against your work) is, in substance, a verification technique already taught, just not named as one.

That naming gap is the track's real story this month, and it has now graduated from a single-source framing suggestion to a confirmed, recurring gap. Last month, one item (the field naming "agentic engineering" the higher-value skill beyond single-prompt crafting) flagged this as a framing opportunity, not a correction. This month supplies two more, independently sourced items making close variants of the same argument. M1 summarizes Simon Willison's argument as: the bottleneck for productive agent use is not instructing AI well, it is confidently verifying that what it did is correct, and reading every line is only one of several verification methods, not the only one (M1 Sweep 2 item 2). MIT Sloan Management Review, in a piece titled "Stop Prompting AI. Start Directing It," argues that carefully directing agents (defining context, capabilities and orientation) produces more original insight than conversational prompting, and that running multiple differently-oriented agents against the same dataset is a genuinely new skill framework distinct from prompt-writing (M1 Sweep 5 item 4). Three independent sources across two months, all converging on the same point (prompting is necessary but insufficient; verification and direction are the higher-value adjacent skills), is no longer vibes. It is a specific, evidenced gap: none of this track's three lessons explicitly teaches verification as its own named discipline, distinct from and sequenced after prompt construction. See Section 4, Gap 1, and Section 5.

### ai-agents (3 lessons, all published)

Lesson 01 ("What Is an AI Agent") and lesson 03 ("When to Use an Agent"), both read in full this cycle, hold without correction. The chatbot-versus-agent distinction, the observe-think-act loop and the "match autonomy to the stakes, keep a human in the loop for consequential actions" governing principle are not contradicted by anything in September's scan.

A genuinely new wrinkle did surface, though, and it is a real gap rather than a confirmation. Ethan Mollick contrasts two models for how autonomous agent systems handle escalation: a fully unreviewed "Software Factory" model versus a "Twilight Factory" model, where a facilitator agent decides, in real time, when a human needs to be looped in (M1 Sweep 2 item 3). Lesson 03's current framework treats the human-in-the-loop threshold as a fixed policy decision a leader sets in advance, by stakes tier, before the agent runs. Mollick's framing describes something meaningfully different: the agent itself exercising judgment, mid-task, about when to escalate, rather than following a pre-set gate. This is not a contradiction of the lesson's core "match autonomy to stakes" principle, a leader still has to decide the stakes and the boundaries, but it names a dynamic, agent-initiated escalation pattern the lesson does not currently address at all. See Section 4, Gap 2.

Salesforce and Anthropic's "Claudeforce" launch, Claude embedded directly in Salesforce with 37 prebuilt sales skills (M1 Sweep 1 item 2), is a concrete, current illustration of lesson 02's subject (how AI connects to tools) worth citing as a fresh, named example, not a correction.
