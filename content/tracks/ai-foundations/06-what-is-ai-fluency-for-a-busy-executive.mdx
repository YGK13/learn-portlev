## Hook

Somebody on your team, or your CEO, or a LinkedIn post you scrolled past, has probably told you to "get AI fluent" this year. Nobody who says it defines it. It sits somewhere between a compliment and an accusation, and either way it is not actionable. You cannot practice a word. You can practice a skill.

Here is the good news: AI fluency is not vague at all once someone names its actual parts. Anthropic, the company that builds Claude, has published its own framework for exactly this, built specifically for people who are not engineers and never plan to be. It breaks down into four competencies, and each one turns into a single, concrete action you can take on real work this week, not a course you enroll in. That is the whole point of this lesson: to turn "get AI fluent" from a vague instruction into four things you can actually do.

<TLDR>
Anthropic's AI Fluency Framework names four competencies: Delegation, Description, Discernment and Diligence. It was built for non-technical people working with AI day to day, not as a coding framework. Delegation is deciding what to hand to AI versus keep for yourself. Description is briefing AI clearly, the way you would brief a sharp new hire. Discernment is knowing where the AI's answer is right and where to override it. Diligence is the habit of checking, iterating and building a repeatable rep. One action per competency, this week, is what "AI fluent" actually means.
</TLDR>

## Context

### Why this is not another coding framework

Most "AI skills" content aimed at executives is secretly written for developers and lightly reworded. Anthropic's AI Fluency Framework is different on purpose: it was built for people whose job is judgment, communication and decisions, not code, and it names the actual skill that separates someone who gets real value from AI from someone who tried it twice and gave up. The framework's own claim, reported through Forbes' 2025 coverage of AI fluency as a defining 2026 leadership trait, is that this is a leadership skill now, not a technical one. That claim matches exactly what this academy already teaches: the gap between you and a fluent AI user is one focused hour of real work, done correctly, not years of study. This lesson names the four things that hour is actually building.

<Pullquote author="Yuri Kruman" source="Closing the AI Wage Gap">
You are learning by doing the work differently, not by adding work on top of work.
</Pullquote>

That line matters here specifically. None of the four competencies below is a new task added to your week. Each one is a way of doing a task you already do, differently.

### The four competencies, defined operationally

**Delegation** is the decision of what to hand to AI versus what you keep for yourself. It is not "give AI everything" or "trust nothing." It is a judgment call, made deliberately, task by task: this first draft, yes; this final call on a person's job, no. Executives who skip this competency either over-delegate (and get burned once, then quit) or under-delegate (and never get the leverage). Fluency means you can name, out loud, why you handed off what you handed off.

**Description** is how clearly you brief the AI. This is the skill this academy's first-hour lesson already teaches as role, context, task and format, and it is worth naming here as its own competency because it is the one most people skip entirely. A vague brief gets a vague answer. A specific brief, the kind you would give a sharp new hire on their first week, gets something you can actually use.

**Discernment** is knowing where the AI's answer is right and where you need to override it. This is judgment, not suspicion. AI is genuinely strong on codified knowledge, the kind of thing written down in a manual or a thousand prior examples, and genuinely weak on anything that depends on your specific relationships, politics or institutional memory. Discernment is the skill of telling those two situations apart in the moment, not after something goes wrong.

<Pullquote author="Yuri Kruman" source="Closing the AI Wage Gap">
The critical skill isn’t knowing how to use AI. It’s knowing where to use it and where to override it.
</Pullquote>

<Callout type="key" title="The test for whether Discernment is working">If you cannot say what you would have overridden in your last AI-assisted piece of work, you did not exercise Discernment. You just accepted the draft.</Callout>

**Diligence** is the habit that makes the other three repeatable instead of a one-time success. It is checking the output before it goes out, iterating instead of accepting the first draft, and, critically, doing the same real task again the next time it comes up so the rep compounds. Diligence is the only one of the four that is a habit rather than a decision, which is why it is the one that actually determines whether "AI fluent" describes you in six months or was true for one afternoon.

<Callout type="example" title="If you lead a team, not just yourself">The same four names work as a teaching tool, not only a personal practice. When someone on your team hands you AI output you do not trust, "walk me through your Discernment on this" is a sharper, less accusatory question than "are you sure about this." When someone over-delegates a call that was theirs to make, "what was your Delegation decision here" names the actual gap. You do not need a training program to start. You need the four words.</Callout>

## Steps

Pick one real, current piece of work for each step. Do not use a test task. The academy's first-hour lesson already made this case: realness is the entire point.

### Step 1: Practice Delegation on one task this week

Choose one task you are already doing this week and make one deliberate call: hand the first draft entirely to AI, or keep it entirely yours, and be able to say why in one sentence. A weekly status summary, a first-pass policy draft or a set of talking points are strong candidates. The goal is not the output. The goal is making the delegation decision on purpose instead of by habit.

### Step 2: Practice Description on the same task

Brief the AI the way you would brief a sharp new analyst: who it should act as, the context it needs, the exact task and the format you want back. If you have not done this before, the first-hour lesson's four-part template (role, context, task, format) is the fastest way in. Notice the difference between what you get from a real brief versus a one-line question.

### Step 3: Practice Discernment on the result

Read what came back and name, specifically, one thing you would keep as-is and one thing you would override or change. Say why for both. This is the rep that builds judgment: not "is this good," but "which part is right because AI is strong at this, and which part needs my specific knowledge that AI does not have."

### Step 4: Practice Diligence by doing it again next week

Pick the same category of task for next week, on purpose. Do not start over from a blank page: reuse the brief that worked, adjusted for the new specifics. The second rep, done more efficiently than the first, is the actual evidence that fluency is forming. If you only ever do this once, you tried AI. If you do it three times running, you are fluent at this one thing, and the next thing gets easier to add.

## Recap

- "AI fluent" is not a vague compliment. Anthropic's own AI Fluency Framework names it as four competencies built for non-technical people: Delegation, Description, Discernment and Diligence.
- Delegation: decide, deliberately, what to hand off and what to keep.
- Description: brief AI like a sharp new hire, with role, context, task and format.
- Discernment: know specifically what you would keep and what you would override, and why.
- Diligence: the habit that turns one good session into a repeatable rep, which is the only one of the four that compounds over time.
- None of this is new work added on top of your week. It is a way of naming the skill you are already building one focused hour at a time.
