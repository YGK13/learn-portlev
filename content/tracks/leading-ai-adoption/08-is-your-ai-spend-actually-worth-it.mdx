## Hook

You already fought the first AI budget battle. You built the business case, named the pilot, got the license approved. That fight is over and you won it. This is a different fight, and it does not end.

The subscriptions you approved a year ago priced AI like a phone plan: one fee, unlimited use, budget it once and move on. That pricing is disappearing. AI vendors are moving to metered billing, a fixed seat fee plus usage charged separately, which means the cost of AI in your organization now rises every time adoption succeeds. The better your team gets at using AI, the bigger the bill. Nobody put that sentence in the original business case.

This lesson is about the decision that follows: not "should we buy AI" but "how much AI use, by whom, on what, is actually worth what it now costs, on an ongoing basis." That is a governance call, and in most organizations right now nobody owns it.

<TLDR>
AI billing is moving from flat, per-seat pricing to metered usage charged on top of a seat fee. That turns AI cost from a one-time approval into a recurring decision that scales with adoption itself. Do not let that decision default to "whoever has a company card." Set a usage envelope tied to the value categories from your original business case, review it on a fixed cadence and name, explicitly, who has the authority to say no.
</TLDR>

## Context

Nickle LaMoreaux, IBM's Chief Human Resources Officer, said it plainly in June 2026: "AI is not free. We act like it's free right now... organizations have to make trade-offs about, 'Do I want people using AI for that or not, is there enough value for the cost?'" That is not a warning from a skeptic. It is the CHRO of one of the world's largest technology companies naming a decision she is personally making, right now, at scale. The same trade-off is arriving at your desk, whether or not you have named it yet.

Here is why the arrival matters more this year than last. The lesson on the business case and ROI in this track walked through how to build a credible cost model before an initiative starts: name the real costs, force every benefit into a countable category, capture a baseline. That work does not expire once the pilot succeeds. It was built for a world of flat, predictable seat costs. Metered usage breaks that assumption, because the cost line is no longer fixed at approval time. It moves every month, in step with how much your team actually uses the tool. A pilot that cost the same amount whether one person used it or fifty now costs meaningfully more at fifty. Success inflates the bill.

That is not a reason to slow adoption down. It is a reason to build a second decision your original business case did not need: an ongoing usage governance call, made on a cadence, by a named owner, using the same value discipline you already applied once. Skip it, and one of two failure modes shows up. Either usage gets rationed by finance anxiety rather than judgment (the tool that was working gets throttled because nobody can explain why the bill grew), or usage grows unchecked until a budget review turns into a crisis (the CFO asks what the AI line item is buying and nobody can answer in specifics). Both are avoidable with the same five-step discipline below.

## Steps

### Step 1: Name the decision as recurring, not one-time

The single biggest mistake is treating the original AI budget approval as the only approval that matters. It was the approval to start. It was never the approval to keep spending at whatever level adoption happens to reach. State this plainly to your finance partner and your team: AI usage cost is now a recurring line, reviewed like headcount or a vendor contract, not a sunk cost from a prior pilot.

### Step 2: Reuse the four value categories, applied to ongoing usage

Go back to the value framework from the business case lesson: time saved, quality improved, capacity created, new capability. Do not build a new framework. Apply the existing one to the question "is this specific, ongoing use still earning its keep," not just "did the pilot work." A workflow that saved four hours a week when one team used it and now costs five times as much because three teams adopted it is not automatically a problem. It is a problem only if the value did not scale with the cost. Check that, by team and by use case, not just in aggregate.

### Step 3: Set a usage envelope, not a blank check

For each team or use case, set an explicit usage ceiling tied to the value it is producing, reviewed at the same cadence as the value check. This is not about restricting good use. It is about making sure growth in spend is a decision someone made on purpose, not a number that arrived on an invoice. A usage envelope with headroom to request more, backed by evidence, beats an unlimited default that nobody is watching.

### Step 4: Put usage review on a fixed calendar, before the bill surprises anyone

Pick a cadence: monthly for fast-scaling use cases, quarterly for stable ones. On that cadence, review actual usage against the envelope and the value it produced. This is the same discipline as the review date and kill criterion from the business case lesson, run again on a shorter loop because metered cost, unlike a flat license, will not wait for your next annual budget cycle to surprise you.

### Step 5: Name who says no

This is the step organizations skip, and it is the one LaMoreaux is describing. Someone has to own the authority to approve, cap or decline an increase in AI usage, in plain language, before the next invoice makes the decision by default. For a CHRO or VP People, this usually means you, for AI touching your function's data and workflows. Write the name down. An unnamed owner is not a neutral outcome. It means finance rations by anxiety, or nobody rations at all.

<Callout type="example">A CHRO who never revisits AI usage cost finds out what it is costing when finance flags the line item at year-end review, with no story ready. A CHRO who reviews usage quarterly against value walks into that same meeting with the answer already built: what it costs, what it returns and why the number is what it is.</Callout>

## Recap

- AI pricing is moving from flat, per-seat fees to metered usage billed on top of a seat fee. That turns AI cost from a one-time approval into a recurring decision that scales with adoption.
- The original business case does not expire, but it was not built for a moving cost line. It needs a second, ongoing discipline: usage governance.
- Reuse the four value categories from your original business case (time saved, quality improved, capacity created, new capability) to judge whether ongoing usage still earns its cost.
- Set a usage envelope per team or use case instead of a blank check, and review it on a fixed cadence tied to the value it is producing.
- Name who has the authority to say no. Left unnamed, the decision defaults to finance anxiety or unchecked growth, neither of which anyone actually chose.
